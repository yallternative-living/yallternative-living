## 1. Risk-ranked checklist

### MUST (fix before the next audit cycle / clear legal exposure or a live litigation trend hitting shops this size)

1. **No gift-card terms anywhere on the site.** `terms.html` never mentions gift cards; `privacy.html` only describes how the balance is *stored*, not the consumer's rights. The federal CARD Act requires point-of-sale disclosure of any expiration date (or a statement that funds don't expire) and of any fees, before purchase — a fee or expiration term that isn't disclosed is unenforceable and must be refunded (15 U.S.C. § 1693l-1; CFPB Reg E subpart D). Since the gift cards appear to never expire (no expiration logic found in the code I read), the fix is cheap: add one clear sentence — no expiration, no dormancy fee, redeemable for merchandise only — to a gift-card terms section, and reference it at the point of sale in the shop's gift-card modal, not just in the privacy policy. *(Sources: 15 U.S.C. §1693l-1, uscode.house.gov; consumercomplianceoutlook.org "Credit CARD Act Requirements for Gift Certificates, Store Gift Cards, and General-Use Prepaid Cards," 2013 but still the controlling federal framework in 2026.)*

2. **California's gift-card cash-back threshold just moved to $15 (SB 22, effective April 1, 2026)** and it applies to *any* seller whose gift card is redeemed by a California resident, not just California-based sellers — this shop ships nationwide, so California cardholders are a near-certainty at any volume. Cal. Civ. Code §1749.5 also bars any expiration date on a purchased card sold to a CA resident. The site needs a line saying balances under $15 are refundable in cash/cash-equivalent on request to CA customers. Low cost, real exposure: CIV §1749.5 claims are a recognized target for CA plaintiffs' firms because the statute has a built-in remedy structure. *(Sources: stradlinglaw.com "California Increases Gift Card Cash-Out Threshold to $15 Starting April 1, 2026"; consumerfinancemonitor.com/2026/03/19 "…plaintiff's attorneys await April 1…"; Cal. Civ. Code §1749.5, law.justia.com.)*

3. **The "Alt-Points" loyalty scheme is decorative, not real — and nothing on the site says so.** `assets/js/main.js` computes and displays point totals per product, but `workers/README.md` states plainly there is no server-side ledger to redeem them against (the old redemption endpoint was deleted for security reasons and never rebuilt). A reasonable shopper reading "Earn X Alt-Points" understands that as an accruing, redeemable benefit — that's the FTC Act §5 "deceptive practice" fact pattern (advertising a benefit that doesn't functionally exist) and every state's mini-FTC-Act (SC's is the SC Unfair Trade Practices Act, S.C. Code §39-5-10 et seq.) tracks it. This isn't a privacy issue, it's a truth-in-advertising one, but it belongs in this list because it's the single highest-severity/lowest-cost fix here: either wire up real redemption, or change the copy to something honest ("track your loyalty status — redemption coming soon") until it is. I did not find a redemption UI anywhere in `main.js`, so I'm treating this as confirmed rather than "maybe."

4. **No documented sales-tax nexus posture, and the Worker's Stripe Tax integration only calculates — it does not register you.** `workers/README.md` step 3 tells the deployer to grant the Stripe key "Tax Settings **read**" so the Worker can tell when Stripe Tax has been turned on in the Dashboard, but activating Stripe Tax in the Dashboard is a separate, manual step, and *even that* only calculates tax in states where you've completed your own state registration — Stripe explicitly does not file the registration paperwork or hold your state tax-portal credentials for you. SC's own economic-nexus threshold is $100,000/yr gross SC sales (no transaction-count leg) — likely irrelevant to a business this size selling *from* SC, since SC nexus for an in-state seller is physical, not economic, and applies from dollar one. The real question is whether this shop has now, or will soon, cross $100k in sales *into* any other single state (many states dropped their 200-transaction leg in 2025–2026, e.g., Alaska, Utah, Illinois, soon Kentucky — leaving revenue as the only trigger, which raises the odds of tripping it almost by accident at a market-and-online mix). At "low thousands of visitors," this is very unlikely today, but it's a live tripwire, not a settled question, and nobody currently owns tracking it. *(Sources: docs.stripe.com/tax/registering — "Stripe does not handle the actual state registration process… businesses must navigate each state's revenue portal… independently"; taxcloud.com "South Carolina Sales Tax Guide 2026" — SC economic nexus $100k/yr, no transaction threshold; avalara.com/blog/2025/06 "States eliminating economic nexus transaction thresholds.")*

5. **Chat-widget wiretap litigation (CIPA and its state-law cousins) is the fastest-growing consumer-facing suit type hitting small e-commerce sites right now, and Tawk.to is exactly the fact pattern being sued over.** Multiple 2026 trackers describe live-chat/chatbot vendors as one of three dominant categories of 2025–2026 California Invasion of Privacy Act (Cal. Penal Code §631/632, "wiretap") suits, alongside session replay and ad pixels — the claim is that the chat vendor (a third party) receives the real-time contents of the conversation without the visitor's consent, which the plaintiffs' bar frames as an unlawful "wiretap" with a **statutory** damages floor of $5,000 per violation (Cal. Penal Code §637.2) — no need to prove actual harm. Filing volume in this exact niche roughly doubled between Dec 2025 and Jan 2026 in the Northern District of California alone, and one tracker counted ~4,000 CA cases by July 2026, with Florida (~800) and Illinois (~110) following under their own wiretap analogs. Tawk.to is currently live on this site (the placeholder-check logic in `terms.html`/`privacy.html`/`policies.html` shows real property/widget IDs already set, not the `YOUR_...` placeholder — so the widget is actually loading on every page). This is genuinely a threat class that has hit shops far smaller than $1M revenue, because plaintiffs' firms scan sites for the vendor script, not for company size. *(Sources: btlaw.com/en/insights/alerts/2026 "CIPA/ECPA Website-Tracking Privacy Litigation in 2026"; insideclassactions.com/2026/01/27 "Website Wiretapping Roundup: 2025 Decisions and Developments"; datagrail.io "CIPA's Second Life.")* Mitigation is cheap relative to the risk: (a) add a pre-chat consent notice / clickwrap before the transcript starts flowing to Tawk.to ("By starting a chat you agree it may be reviewed by Tawk.to, our chat provider — see our Privacy Policy"), which is the standard defense pattern being adopted across the industry in 2026, and (b) confirm the privacy policy names Tawk.to as a recipient of chat content specifically (right now `privacy.html`'s data-recipients list — lines ~252–256 — names Cloudflare and Resend but I didn't find Tawk.to called out by name anywhere in the file; that omission itself is worth closing even independent of the wiretap angle, since CCPA-style "categories of third parties" disclosure expects it).

### SHOULD (meaningfully reduces exposure; not urgent, but don't let it sit past a policy refresh)

6. **ADA Title III web-accessibility risk is real but proportionate — don't over-invest, don't ignore it either.** 2025 federal filings hit ~3,117 (a 27% jump over 2024, second-highest year on record), and >5,000 when state-court filings are counted; NY, FL, and CA remain the top venues, with Illinois newly surging (28→585 cases in a year). The demographic is shifting slightly upmarket (36% of H1-2025 defendants had >$25M revenue, up from 33% in 2024), meaning the plaintiffs' bar is *not* exclusively hunting micro-shops, but it hasn't stopped hunting them either — there's no small-business carve-out in Title III, unlike the privacy statutes. There's no binding regulatory text requiring WCAG for private business sites (the DOJ's 2024 WCAG 2.1 AA mandate is Title II — state/local government — only), but WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA is the de facto standard courts import into Title III settlements and consent decrees. Realistic posture for this shop: a baseline accessibility pass (alt text, contrast, keyboard nav, form labels, the search modal's `aria-*` wiring already looks reasonably good from what I read) is worth doing opportunistically, but a $250/mo accessibility-overlay subscription is not proportionate spend at this scale — those overlay widgets are themselves increasingly cited *as evidence of bad faith* in litigation when they paper over real defects. *(Sources: adatitleiii.com/2026/03 "Federal Court Website Accessibility Lawsuit Filings Bounce Back in 2025"; levelaccess.com "2026 Trends"; americanbar.org/2025-august "Digital Accessibility Under Title III of the ADA.")*

7. **No comprehensive state privacy law currently applies to this business** (see table below) — but the reasoning is worth writing down once so it doesn't have to be re-derived every audit cycle, and worth re-checking annually, because thresholds move (see #10 below, Delaware just proposed cutting its threshold in half). This isn't a defect in the current policy — `privacy.html`'s honest "we're a tiny handmade business… we'll do our honest best" framing (lines 273–274) is arguably *better* than a boilerplate CCPA notice that overclaims coverage the site doesn't actually have and creates rights/obligations that then go unfulfilled.

8. **CAN-SPAM basics look mostly fine but verify two mechanical points live, not just in principle.** The site uses Kit, whose forms support unsubscribe and consent tooling, but CAN-SPAM compliance is the *sender's* legal responsibility regardless of platform (FTC guidance is explicit both parties can be liable). Confirm (a) every marketing email — welcome, birthday-club $5 voucher, restock alert — carries a working, one-click unsubscribe and a real physical address in the footer (Kit templates do this by default but it's worth eyeballing one actual sent email, not just the signup form), and (b) unsubscribe requests are honored within CAN-SPAM's required window (no fee, no login, no more than an email address required). Civil penalty exposure is nominally up to ~$53k *per email*, though FTC enforcement against solo operators at this scale is not the realistic risk — the CAN-SPAM private right of action is narrow (mostly ISPs), so this is a "don't be sloppy" item, not a "someone is about to sue you" item. *(Source: ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business.)*

9. **The "10% off first order" + birthday-field popup is consent-adjacent, not currently a compliance gap, but worth a sentence of hygiene.** The birthday field is optional, MM/DD only (no year, no COPPA-triggering birth-year-driven age math), and the form posts directly to Kit rather than routing through anything that infers age. That's a genuinely good design choice — it minimizes the COPPA exposure that a full DOB field would create. The one gap: the footer signup form doesn't show a link to the privacy policy *at the point of collection* (I read the form markup in `privacy.html`/`terms.html`/`policies.html` and didn't see a policy link adjacent to the email/birthday fields) — CalOPPA (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §22575) and general FTC "notice at collection" expectations favor a policy link right there, not just in the footer three rows down. Cheap fix, not urgent.

10. **Escheat/unclaimed-property exposure on unredeemed gift-card balances looks low today but track it.** South Carolina's unclaimed-property statute (S.C. Code Title 27, Chapter 18) is generally read as **not** covering gift certificates specifically (confirmed via multiple secondary sources, though I did not pull the primary statutory text directly — flagged below as unverified-with-primary-source). If that reading holds, SC-domiciled balances likely don't need annual escheat reporting. But: (a) other states' escheat laws attach based on the *last known address of the cardholder*, not the seller's state, and several states (unlike SC) *do* sweep gift-card balances after a dormancy period — so if this shop ever collects customer addresses tied to gift-card purchases, a small compliance surface opens up state-by-state; (b) SC's general unclaimed-property due-diligence rule (report/remit by Oct 31, notify holders of balances ≥$50) is a real annual-calendar item worth just knowing exists even if gift cards themselves are out of scope. *(Sources: dropzone.marketing "Escheat and Gift Card Laws: South Carolina"; sovos.com "South Carolina Escheat & Unclaimed Property Laws" — both secondary; I was not able to independently confirm §39-1-55's escheat-exemption language against the primary S.C. Code text in this session, so treat the "gift cards are exempt from SC escheat" conclusion as probable-but-not-verified.)*

### WATCH (low current risk at this size / traffic — track it, don't build for it yet)

11. **State comprehensive privacy laws generally won't reach this business at current scale — see the applicability table below** — but the trend line is toward lower thresholds (Delaware's HB-380 would cut 35,000→15,000 consumers; Connecticut just cut 100,000→35,000 and added no-threshold triggers for anyone who *sells* personal data or processes *sensitive* data at all, regardless of volume). "Consumers" in these statutes generally means state residents whose personal data you *process*, which can include site visitors tracked via cookies/analytics, not just paying customers — so growth in *traffic*, not just sales, is the metric to watch, and Umami's cookieless design already helps keep that count down structurally.

12. **Texas and Nebraska have no consumer-count floor at all**, but both exempt anyone who qualifies as an SBA "small business" (Texas TDPSA; Nebraska NDPA) — SBA size standards for the relevant NAICS codes (soap/cosmetics manufacturing, or online retail) run from several million to tens of millions in receipts, so a sub-$1M shop is unambiguously under that bar today. The one carve-out inside the carve-out: even an SBA-small business in TX/NE cannot sell *sensitive* personal data without consent, regardless of size — almost certainly moot here since nothing in the stack (Stripe, Kit, Umami, Tawk.to, Formspree) looks like a data-sale relationship, but worth naming so it's on record as considered.

13. **TCPA/SMS is not currently in play** (no SMS marketing found in the code I reviewed) — flagging only because the prompt named it as a possible future addition. If SMS is ever added: TCPA requires prior express written consent for marketing texts (a checkbox that isn't pre-checked, with clear disclosure of message frequency and "msg & data rates may apply," separate from any general newsletter opt-in), a working STOP keyword, and — increasingly relevant in 2025-2026 — carrier-side "10DLC" registration to avoid messages being silently filtered. This is a "build it right the first time" item, not a current gap.

14. **FTC Mail Order Rule (16 CFR Part 435) applies today and looks broadly consistent with current site copy**, but it's worth a one-line gap check: the Rule requires a *reasonable basis* to expect shipment within the advertised window (or 30 days if none is stated), and — critically — a specific delay-notice-and-consent mechanism if that slips: notify the customer, give a revised date, and if the new date is >30 days out or indefinite, the customer's *silence* does **not** count as consent — you must cancel and refund unless they affirmatively agree to wait. `policies.html`'s current shipping section (lines ~204-207 area) says processing times "vary by product" and points to the product page, which is fine as a baseline, but there's no stated mechanism for what happens if a batch runs *over* its stated processing time — worth a sentence, since this is one of the few consumer-protection rules that's squarely mandatory federal law with real FTC teeth, not just plaintiff-bar litigation risk. *(Source: ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule; ecfr.gov 16 CFR Part 435.)*

15. **FTC's "Click to Cancel" negative-option rule is currently vacated** (8th Circuit, July 8 2025, on procedural grounds — failure to do a required cost-benefit analysis) and the FTC opened a new rulemaking (ANPRM) in March 2026 to try again. Not applicable regardless: this shop has no subscriptions/auto-renewals today. Pure watch item — relevant only if a "subscribe & save" salve/soak subscription is ever added, at which point ROSCA (the underlying federal negative-option statute, which survived the vacatur) and state auto-renewal laws (CA, NY, and others have their own independent auto-renewal statutes that don't depend on the FTC rule) would apply regardless of the rule's status. *(Sources: sidley.com "US FTC Click-to-Cancel Rule Struck Down"; wilmerhale.com "…Federal and State Regulators Likely to Remain Active.")*

16. **"Free shipping over $X" claims are fine under FTC rules as currently written on the site** — I did not find a live "free shipping over $40" claim in the pages/data I grep'd (may exist elsewhere, e.g. cart UI copy I didn't fully trace); if/when such a claim exists, the FTC's baseline rule is simple: if shipping is excluded from the advertised "free" price, the threshold/condition must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously before checkout, not buried — a cart-page banner already tends to satisfy this. Not a current finding, just a rule to keep in mind if that promo copy is added or changed.

## 3. "What would change my mind" — re-run this analysis if any of these happen

- **Traffic grows past ~25,000 unique visitors/year whose data is processed** (even without a purchase) — that's the low end of the "consumer" thresholds in the newer/stricter states (DE, MD, NH). At that point, re-check Delaware, Maryland, New Hampshire, and Connecticut specifically, since they have the lowest floors and Connecticut has no-threshold triggers for sensitive-data or sale-of-data activity that don't depend on volume at all.
- **Any data-sale or ad-retargeting relationship gets added** (a Meta Pixel, a Google Ads conversion feed with PII matching, an affiliate/data-sharing deal) — several states (CO, CT) drop or bypass their volume threshold entirely once *any* personal data is "sold" or shared for cross-context advertising, and "sale" is defined broadly enough in some statutes to catch some ad-tech integrations that don't look like a sale in plain English.
- **The business crosses ~$8-25M revenue** — irrelevant at current scale, but this is the point several revenue-gated laws (CA, UT, TN) start to bite, and it's also roughly the range where "SBA small business" stops being an automatic exemption for TX/NE, so Texas and Nebraska should be re-examined together at that point.
- **Sales into any single state cross $100k/year** — re-run the sales-tax nexus analysis for that specific state and confirm Stripe Tax is both calculating *and* that a state registration actually exists (Stripe won't tell you if you forgot to register — it will silently calculate $0 tax instead, per Stripe's own documentation).
- **SMS marketing, a subscription/auto-ship product, or a loyalty-point *redemption* feature gets built** — TCPA, state auto-renewal statutes, and (for redemption) state gift-card/stored-value-instrument rules would all newly apply; none of them apply to the current *display-only* points feature, but the moment redemption is real, it starts looking like a stored-value instrument in several states.
- **A Delaware-style threshold cut (35k→15k) actually passes**, or another state follows Connecticut's no-threshold-for-sensitive-data model — worth a light annual scan of privacy-law trackers (IAPP's state tracker or similar) rather than re-deriving this whole table from scratch each time.
- **A CIPA-style suit gets filed against a similarly-sized shop using Tawk.to specifically** (rather than the broader "chat vendors" category) — that would move item #5 from "watch this litigation trend" to "this is a live pattern targeting your exact vendor," and would justify moving faster than "next policy refresh."

## 4. Section-by-section detail (supporting the checklist above)

### 4a. Email marketing (CAN-SPAM, Kit, consent, SMS/TCPA)
CAN-SPAM (15 U.S.C. §7701 et seq., enforced via 16 CFR Part 316) requires: no false/misleading header or subject info; clear identification of the message as an ad (implicit is fine for a retailer's own newsletter to its own list); a valid physical postal address; a clear, working opt-out mechanism honored within 10 business days with no fee/login/extra-info requirement; and the opt-out must keep working for at least 30 days after send. Penalties are nominally ~$53,088 per violating email after inflation adjustment, but CAN-SPAM's private right of action is narrow (largely limited to ISPs and, in some states, state AGs) — realistic enforcement risk for a solo operator is low, but the mechanics should still just work correctly, since state UDAP statutes (SC's included) can independently reach deceptive email practices even where CAN-SPAM's federal remedy doesn't. Kit (formerly ConvertKit) is GDPR/CCPA-compliant as a *processor/service provider* and offers a DPA, consent checkboxes, and double opt-in tooling — none of that automatically makes the *sender's* campaigns compliant; that responsibility stays with the shop. The "10% off first order" + optional birthday popup is a reasonable, low-risk consent pattern (opt-in, not pre-checked, no birth year collected) — the one gap is no policy link directly at the point of collection (see checklist #9). SMS/TCPA doesn't currently apply (no SMS found) — see checklist #13 for what applies if added.

### 4b. Digital gift cards
Federal: CARD Act / Reg E Subpart D bars expiration under 5 years from purchase/last load (or requires clear disclosure if shorter — inapplicable if the cards simply don't expire, which appears to be the design here) and requires disclosure of any fees before purchase; inactivity fees (if ever added) can't apply until 12 months of dormancy and are capped at one per month. South Carolina: S.C. Code §39-1-55 bars expiration before the 1-year anniversary of sale unless disclosed — moot if cards don't expire at all, which is the more consumer-friendly and simpler choice already apparently in effect. California: Civ. Code §1749.5 bars any expiration date on a card sold to a CA resident, and (as of April 1, 2026, per SB 22) requires cash-back on request for balances under $15. Escheat: SC likely doesn't sweep unredeemed gift-card balances (see checklist #10, flagged as probable-not-verified); other states' escheat exposure depends on cardholder address, a genuinely low-probability-but-nonzero surface at this scale. **Net: the fix is not "redesign the gift card program," it's "write down, in one visible place, the terms that are already true in practice"** — no expiration, no fees, CA cash-back-under-$15 on request, redeemable for merchandise/services only, non-transferable if that's the intent.

### 4c. Sales tax
South Carolina nexus is physical (business is located there) — sales-tax collection duty on in-state operations exists regardless of any threshold; SC's $100k economic-nexus threshold governs *remote sellers with no SC presence* selling into SC, which is the inverse situation and not relevant here. For sales *out* of SC into other states, economic nexus is the live question — historically $100k/yr and/or 200 transactions per state, but the 200-transaction leg is being dropped by more states each year (16 states as of Jan 2026 per Avalara), leaving revenue-only triggers, which somewhat *lowers* the bar to trip nexus by transaction count alone (a shop with many small transactions could hit 200 orders in a state well before $100k in sales, under the old dual-threshold model — but as that leg disappears, only the dollar figure matters going forward, which for many small-ticket handmade-goods shops is actually a higher effective bar than before). Stripe Tax calculates but does not register — states require the seller to independently obtain a sales-tax permit before Stripe Tax will collect anything for that state (confirmed directly from Stripe's own docs). Marketplace-facilitator rules (relevant to the Etsy side of this business, which the LocalBusiness schema/footer confirms exists) generally shift collection duty to Etsy for Etsy-facilitated sales — direct site sales via the Cloudflare Worker + Stripe remain the shop's own collection responsibility, a genuinely different legal posture between the two channels worth keeping straight.

### 4d. Consumer protection (FTC Mail Order Rule, refund policy disclosure, free shipping, auto-renewal/Click-to-Cancel)
Covered in checklist items #14–16 above. One item not yet mentioned: **California (Civ. Code §1723) and New York (GBL §218-a) both effectively *require* a posted return/refund policy** — if a retailer doesn't conspicuously post one, the legal default becomes a full refund within 30 days regardless of what the retailer would have preferred. `policies.html` already has a clear, public return/exchange policy, which satisfies this in substance — the "conspicuous" requirement in CA specifically contemplates point-of-sale posting (signage/tags/order forms) in a brick-and-mortar context, which translates online to "on the site, reachable from checkout," already true here via the footer link. Good as-is; no action needed beyond what's already there.

### 4e. Accessibility (ADA Title III)
