# PDP decision: single shop page + modal vs. real per-product pages

**Question:** For a 19-SKU handmade body-care shop (Etsy + own static site, Stripe Checkout, mostly mobile traffic), should the site keep one shop page with a quick-view modal and catalogue-wide `ItemList` JSON-LD, with per-product URLs existing only as `noindex` redirect stubs for social unfurls (Option A) — or build real, indexable, self-canonical product detail pages with per-product `Product` JSON-LD, in the sitemap (Option B)?

## Recommendation

**Build Option B** — 19 real, indexable, self-canonical PDPs generated from the existing JSON at build time — modified with one hybrid concession: **keep the modal too**, wired to open from the shop page via `history.pushState`-driven deep links into the same PDP URLs, so mobile shoppers still get an in-page quick-look while every product still owns a crawlable, shareable, canonical URL. This is not a close call. Google's own documentation states plainly that product rich results (both "merchant listing" and "product snippet" experiences) **only support pages that focus on a single product**, and explicitly disqualifies category-style pages ("shoes in our shop") from that eligibility — so Option A's catalogue-wide `ItemList` JSON-LD is structurally ineligible for the rich results it's ostensibly built to earn. Separately, Google Merchant Center's landing-page rules forbid gating product info behind an action, and require the page not be blocked by `noindex` — which is exactly what Option A's stub pages are. OpenAI's Agentic Commerce product-feed spec requires a "Product detail page URL" as a mandatory field per product. At 19 SKUs, none of Option B's usual costs (thin content at scale, crawl budget, build maintenance) are real problems: the pages are templated from JSON you already maintain, and 19 URLs is two orders of magnitude below where Google says crawl budget starts to matter. The only genuine cost — diluting "shop.html" as a single strong page into 19 thinner ones — is manageable with a substantive template (materials, care instructions, scent notes, shipping/return policy per product) rather than a 300-character blurb, and is outweighed by the eligibility, shareability, and agentic-commerce benefits.

## 1. Google Search Central: product structured data (2025–2026)

**Single-product-page requirement is explicit and stated identically in two separate current docs.** The Product Snippet guidelines (last updated 2025-12-10) state under Technical Guidelines:

> "product rich results only support pages that focus on a single product (or multiple variants of the same product)."
[Product snippet structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/product-snippet)

The Merchant Listing guidelines repeat the identical requirement and give a concrete disqualifying example:

> "Product rich results only support pages that focus on a single product (or multiple variants of the same product)." Pages like "shoes in our shop" are ineligible. "Only pages where a shopper can purchase a product are eligible for merchant listing experiences, not pages with links to other sites that sell the product."
[Merchant listing structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/merchant-listing)

This is a documented requirement, not a best-practice opinion: a single `shop.html` carrying `Product`/`Offer` JSON-LD for 19 different items in an `ItemList` does not qualify a page for the Product rich-result / merchant-listing experience, because the page's focus is a catalogue, not one product. Adding an `ItemList` of `Product` items doesn't change this — it changes the wrapper type, not the "single-product focus" test Google applies to the page itself.

**Product snippets vs. merchant listings:** Product snippets are for pages "where people can't directly purchase the product" (e.g., editorial reviews); merchant listings are for pages "where customers can purchase products from you" — which is the shop's case, since it uses Stripe Checkout directly. Both share the single-product-page constraint above. ("Adding the required product information properties for merchant listings means your product pages can also be eligible for product snippets" — [Intro to Product Structured Data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/product), last updated 2025-12-10.)

**Variants (`ProductGroup`/`hasVariant`):** Google's dedicated page (last updated 2026-05-20) supports both patterns — variants selectable via query params on one canonical URL ("single page website"), or variants on genuinely separate pages ("multi page website," where "this doesn't apply since there is no single canonical URL representing the ProductGroup"). This is irrelevant to Y'allternative's core question — the 19 products aren't variants of one product — but confirms Google's structured-data model is built around *one canonical URL per purchasable unit* (product or variant), never a shared catalogue URL for unrelated items.
[Product Variant Structured Data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/product-variants)

**`ItemList`/Carousel on a listing page:** Google does have a mechanism for multi-item pages — the Carousel (beta) feature — but it doesn't rescue Option A. Per the Carousel docs (last updated 2025-12-10): an "all-in-one-page" carousel is allowed, but each item must carry its **full** type-specific schema (not just `position`/`url`), all items must be "of the same type," and — critically — the general structured-data policy (`sd-policies`, last updated 2026-07-10) requires:

> "Don't mark up content that is not visible to readers of the page... Don't block your structured data pages to Googlebot using robots.txt, `noindex`, or any other access control methods."
[Carousel structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/carousel) / [General Structured Data Guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies)

Two problems for Option A: (a) Carousel is a distinct rich-result type from Product/Merchant-listing rich results — it doesn't grant product-snippet or merchant-listing eligibility, it's a separate host-carousel UI; and (b) if the individual product URLs referenced by the `ItemList` are `noindex` stubs, that's the exact configuration the policy calls out — content whose supporting page is blocked from indexing.

## 2. Google Merchant Center / free listings landing-page requirements

Merchant Center's `[link]` attribute rules ([support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324416](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324416?hl=en)) require, among other things:

> "Don't require users to register, sign in, or take any other actions to view information about your product." Best practice: "Link to your landing page with the correct variant selected. Users who view the product they clicked on are more likely to purchase it."

A page that requires clicking a quick-view trigger to reveal the specific product's price/details is exactly the kind of "action required to view product information" this discourages, even if it doesn't use the word "modal." Separately, the Merchant Listing structured-data page states the destination page "must be accessible to Google and not blocked by a robots.txt file, the `noindex` tag, or login requirements" — Option A's stub pages are `noindex` by design, so they are disqualified from merchant-listing eligibility outright, independent of the modal question. The shop isn't necessarily enrolled in Merchant Center free listings today, but this is the documented bar for that channel if/when they are, and the underlying `noindex` disqualification also applies to plain Search's Merchant Listing / product-snippet rich results (same requirement appears in the general policy doc — section 1 above).

## 3. AI shopping agents and answer engines (2026)

**OpenAI Agentic Commerce (ChatGPT Shopping):** The official spec (developers.openai.com, current as of the 2026-01-30 stable spec version referenced across vendor guides) lists a `url` field described as **"Product detail page URL"** and marked **Required** for every feed row, with the constraint that it "must resolve with HTTP 200." OpenAI's best-practices doc adds only feed-hygiene notes (encode URLs, use UTM params) and doesn't specify on-page requirements beyond that.
[Product Feed Spec – Agentic Commerce](https://developers.openai.com/commerce/product-feeds/spec?fields=required), [Best practices – Agentic Commerce](https://developers.openai.com/commerce/guides/best-practices)

This is feed-based discovery (merchants push a feed to OpenAI directly), not page-crawling — so in principle Option A could populate a feed with 19 "product detail page URLs" pointing at `noindex` stubs. But two things argue against relying on that: the field is literally named "product detail page," and a 200-status page that then noindexes itself and JS-redirects is a fragile, unusual target to hand a merchant-feed validator that's checking resolvability — it's an edge case the spec plainly wasn't written to accommodate, versus a real PDP which trivially satisfies it.

**Google's Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP)**, per Google's own announcement (summarized secondhand via [The Keyword](https://www.thekeyword.co/news/google-publishes-help-page-for-universal-commerce-protocol), which reports on Google's help page), lets AI agents "discover a product, confirm inventory and pricing, review policies, and initiate checkout" through Merchant Center feeds/APIs/on-page markup — again feed-first, but its checkout path assumes the merchant's product data (and by extension, product identity) maps to something an agent can present and link back to. A dedicated PDP is the natural anchor; a modal-only product has no independent identity to hand back to the user ("here's the product," with a link) once the agent leaves the feed context.

**Perplexity, AI Overviews / AI Mode, and Bing/Copilot shopping:** I could not find first-party Perplexity documentation (only third-party SEO/marketing guides, which I'm treating as lower-confidence secondary sources — e.g. [rewarx.com](https://www.rewarx.com/blogs/perplexity-shopping-ecommerce-guide), [verityscore.io](https://verityscore.io/en/kb/perplexity-shopping/)). These consistently describe Perplexity as ingesting **Schema.org `Product`/`Offer`/`AggregateRating` markup via its own crawler (PerplexityBot)** in addition to merchant feeds, and note that a blocked `robots.txt` prevents citation. If Option A's individual product URLs are `noindex`, they may still be crawlable (noindex ≠ disallow) but are explicitly telling every crawler "don't index this" — a signal AI answer engines have no strong reason to override, since the whole point of `noindex` is "don't treat this as a citable page." Google's own AI Overviews guidance states no special structured data is required for AI features, but that AI surfaces draw on the same rich-result eligibility as classic Search — reinforcing that Section 1's single-product-page requirement is also the gate for AI-surfaced product results, not just classic blue-link snippets.

**Net for Section 3:** the emerging pattern across OpenAI, Google (UCP), and (per secondary sources) Perplexity is "feed tells the agent about a product; a real per-product URL is the anchor/landing target for it." None of them are designed around, or accommodate well, a catalogue page whose product detail is only reachable via a client-side modal state that has no server-rendered, indexable URL.

## 4. Conversion evidence: quick-view/modal vs. full PDP, especially mobile

This is the softest evidence category — I could not find recent controlled A/B data specifically isolating "quick-view modal vs. dedicated PDP" with clean percentages; most CRO case studies are about unrelated funnel changes. What's documented:

- **Baymard Institute** ([Provide "Quick Views" for Visually Driven Products](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-desktop-quick-views), Aug 9 2022) found quick-view "typically test[s] well on sites that have insufficient information on the list view" — i.e., it's a fix for a specific defect (thin list-view info), not a universal win — and reported usability failures: users mistaking the quick-view for the actual PDP, difficulty returning to the same scroll position in the list on mobile, and (notably) that **"almost all big ecommerce sites drop the quick view from their mobile versions"** despite the site being explicitly mobile-driven traffic for Y'allternative.
- Baymard's companion research, "[Avoid 'Quick View' Overlays](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-quick-views)" (via secondary summary, original behind Baymard's paywall), concludes quick-view "add[s] an additional layer of friction... without adding substantial UX benefit," with users repeatedly clicking the overlay by mistake when trying to reach the real product image/page, and recommends **hover-triggered inline info instead of a modal** as the better-tested pattern.
- Nielsen Norman Group maintains a large paid report specifically on ecommerce PDPs ("[Ecommerce UX: Product Pages](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-product-pages-including-reviews/)," 108 guidelines) but its content is paywalled; I can confirm its existence and general thesis (dedicated product pages need substantial standalone information) but not quote specifics — flagging this as unverified beyond the report's public abstract.
- No Shopify/BigCommerce first-party benchmark data isolating quick-view lift was found; general Shopify conversion/add-to-cart benchmarks (1.4% CVR average, 4.6% add-to-cart average per 2026 aggregator data) exist but don't bear on this specific question and are omitted as irrelevant.

**Deep-linkability for sharing/ads/email/Etsy cross-linking** is the stronger, more structural point, though evidenced more by absence-of-capability than by a benchmark study: a `noindex` stub redirect is explicitly built for "social unfurls" per the prompt's own framing, meaning the team already recognizes Option A can't do sharing without a workaround. But redirect stubs solve *unfurling*, not: paid-ads landing-page relevance (Google/Meta ad quality scoring rewards a landing page that matches the ad's specific product), email campaigns linking to one salve restock, or an Etsy listing's "more from this shop" or bio-link pointing at a specific product rather than a generic shop page. A real PDP is a strictly more capable version of the stub for every one of these uses, at no extra runtime cost since it's statically generated.

## 5. Costs of Option B for a 19-SKU catalogue

- **Thin/duplicate content risk:** Real, but self-inflicted, not structural. The risk is a 300-character blurb duplicated with only a product name swapped — that *would* read as templated thin content. The fix is a page template with genuinely per-product content (ingredients/materials, scent or use notes, care instructions, size/weight, shipping specifics) — normal PDP content most handmade-goods shops already have somewhere (Etsy listing descriptions are a ready source to adapt). At 19 pages this is a few hours of copywriting, not an ongoing burden, since the static-site generator already builds pages from the existing product JSON (`assets/data/events.json`-style data pipeline visible in this repo's `scripts/build-site-data.js`).
- **Crawl budget:** Google's own guidance states crawl-budget management is for "large sites (1 million+ unique pages)... or medium/larger sites (10,000+ unique pages) with very rapidly changing content," and explicitly: "If your site doesn't have a large number of pages that change rapidly... you don't need to read this guide." ([Large site owner's guide to managing crawl budget](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/large-site-managing-crawl-budget), last updated 2026-07-22.) 19 pages is roughly 0.002% of the threshold Google itself cites as a rough floor for concern. This cost is not real at this scale.
- **Maintenance burden:** Low, because the repo already treats product data as structured JSON consumed by a build script (per the `git status` context: `scripts/build-site-data.js`, `assets/data/events.json`, `assets/js/events-data.js` are all mid-refactor in this repo already). Generating one static HTML file per product from that same JSON at build time is the same category of work as generating the existing `events.json`-driven pages — not a new engineering pattern for this codebase.
- **Splitting ranking signals between `shop.html` and PDPs:** This is the one legitimate trade-off. A single strong page can consolidate backlinks/authority; splitting into 19 pages means each has less signal individually, and internal linking (shop page → each PDP, each PDP → shop page and related products) has to be done deliberately or the PDPs will be weak orphans. At 19 products, this is manageable with straightforward internal linking (grid of cards on `shop.html`, each linking to its PDP, each PDP linking back and to 2-3 related products) — but it does mean `shop.html` should keep being the primary target for generic "shop" queries while PDPs target specific-product and long-tail queries (e.g., a specific salve name), which is exactly the query-segmentation dedicated PDPs are for.

## 6. Counter-evidence: is single-page-catalogue defensible for a micro-shop?

I looked specifically for 2025-2026 sources defending single-page catalogues for small shops and found weaker support than expected:

- The clearest "one page is enough" pattern in current sources ([Shopify: 15 Single-Product Website Examples](https://www.shopify.com/blog/single-product-website), [Network Solutions: 25 single-product ecommerce examples](https://www.networksolutions.com/blog/single-product-ecommerce-website/)) is specifically about **single-product** brands (one SKU, one page, e.g. a single smart-fridge or bidet company) — a fundamentally different situation from a 19-SKU multi-category catalogue. That pattern doesn't transfer: it works *because* there's only one product to disambiguate, which is the opposite of Y'allternative's situation (salves vs. bath soaks vs. body butter vs. sprays vs. apparel vs. gift card).
- General small-business SEO commentary for 2026 (aggregated, non-primary sources) leans the other way — emphasizing "one topic per page" and dedicated pages per offering as the standard recommendation, not single-page consolidation.
- I found no credible primary source (Google, a recognized UX research body, or a platform vendor) explicitly arguing that a single catalogue page with a modal is *sufficient or preferable* for a multi-SKU shop in 2025-2026. The nearest thing — Baymard's finding that quick-view "tests well" when list-view info is insufficient — is a narrow claim about a specific defect fix, not an endorsement of forgoing PDPs altogether; Baymard's own broader guidance (Section 4) leans toward avoiding overlay-only product info.

## What would change my mind

- If Google, OpenAI, or Perplexity published documentation stating that `ItemList`/`Product` JSON-LD embedded in a single catalogue page is *itself* eligible for Product rich results or agentic-commerce indexing (contradicting the "single product focus" language currently in Google's docs) — i.e., if the stated single-page requirement in Sections 1–2 were reversed or qualified away.
- If Y'allternative's actual traffic data showed negligible organic/AI-referral traffic to product-level queries (i.e., all traffic is Etsy-driven or direct/social to the shop page already) — in that case, the marginal SEO/AI-discoverability upside of PDPs would matter less, though the sharing/ads/email deep-linking case would still stand on its own.
- If maintaining 19 additional templated pages turned out, in practice, to require substantially more than a few hours (e.g., if the build pipeline genuinely couldn't template them from existing JSON without a larger refactor) — that would shift the cost side of the calculus, though nothing in the repo's current `scripts/build-site-data.js` pattern suggests that's the case.
- Controlled conversion-rate data (not found in this research) directly showing PDPs *reduce* mobile conversion for a catalogue this size relative to a modal-only flow — Baymard's evidence points the other way but is not a clean same-shop A/B test.
