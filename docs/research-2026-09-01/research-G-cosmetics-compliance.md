## 1. FDA cosmetic vs. drug line — the core issue

**[LAW]** Under the FD&C Act, a "cosmetic" is something intended to cleanse, beautify, or alter appearance; a "drug" is anything intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease, **or to affect the structure or function of the body** — regardless of whether the ingredients are natural/plant-derived. Intended use is read from *label claims, website copy, advertising, keywords/SEO metadata, and even consumer expectation* — not just the physical jar label. (FDA, "Is It a Cosmetic, a Drug, or Both? (Or Is It Soap?)," updated 9/11/2024, https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-laws-regulations/it-cosmetic-drug-or-both-or-it-soap)

FDA's own examples that map directly onto her line:
- *"A massage oil... intended to lubricate the skin and impart fragrance is a cosmetic, but if the product is intended for a therapeutic use, such as relieving muscle pain, it's a drug."*
- *"A fragrance marketed with certain 'aromatherapy' claims, such as assertions that the scent will help the consumer sleep..., meets the definition of a drug."*
(Same URL above.)

FDA's Aromatherapy page is even more explicit: *"claims that a product will relieve colic, ease pain, relax muscles, treat depression or anxiety, or help you sleep are drug claims."* (FDA, "Aromatherapy," updated 9/8/2023, https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/aromatherapy)

**Real enforcement examples for small/handmade sellers** — Marie Gale (industry labeling consultant who tracks FDA warning letters against handcrafted soap/cosmetic makers) compiled letters citing exactly this pattern: *"Arnica – Reduces swelling"*, testimonials repeating drug claims, and — closest to this brand — a market vendor selling what was essentially a salt/oil scrub under a banner reading **"Helps with Eczema & Psoriasis, No More Cracked Heels"** ("FDA Warning Letters – Drug Claims for Cosmetics," https://www.mariegale.com/fda-warning-letters-drug-claims-for-cosmetics/). A 2024 FDA warning letter to Cosmetic Solutions LLC also flagged a topical product formulated with eucalyptus oil and "cell repair" language as an unapproved external-analgesic drug (FDA, MARCS-CMS 670486, 5/31/2024, https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/cosmetic-solutions-llc-670486-05312024). Older letters against small "CBD salve" sellers cite claims like *"reduce pain and inflammation associated with... arthritis"* and *"relieve neuropathy"* almost word-for-word matching her Miracle Balm keywords (FDA warning letters to Infinite CBD, 11/22/2019, and Cannafyl, 3/1/2021).

## 2. MoCRA — what she must actually do in 2026

**[LAW]** MoCRA (Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022) added several obligations to the FD&C Act. Key 2026 status, from FDA's own guidance and recent legal-industry tracking of the Unified Agenda:

**Facility registration & product listing — exempt, with exceptions.**
Businesses with average U.S. gross annual cosmetics sales under $1,000,000 (3-year average) are exempt from the facility registration and product listing requirements — but *only if none of their products* fall into these carve-outs (FDA fact sheet, "Small Businesses & Homemade Cosmetics," content current 11/18/2025, https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/resources-industry-cosmetics/small-businesses-homemade-cosmetics-fact-sheet):
- Products that regularly contact the mucous membrane of the eye under customary use,
- Products that are injected,
- Products intended for internal use,
- Products intended to alter appearance for more than 24 hours where removal isn't part of customary use.

None of her current products fall into these categories (no eye-area, injectable, ingestible, or long-duration-alteration products), and her stated revenue is well under $1M, so **[OPINION]** she should currently qualify for the exemption from facility registration and product listing. **A single future SKU** in one of those excepted categories (e.g., a long-wear tattoo-style body art item, an eye product) **would trigger full registration/listing obligations for the whole business** — worth flagging before she launches anything eye-area or long-wear.

**What she is NOT exempt from, regardless of size [LAW]:**
- **Adverse event recordkeeping and reporting.** Since Dec 29, 2023, "responsible persons" must record and keep all adverse event reports (not just serious ones), and report *serious* adverse events to FDA within **15 business days** of receipt (with a 15-business-day follow-up window for new medical information). Small businesses/distributors need only retain records for **3 years** rather than the standard 6. (Registrar Corp summary of MoCRA AE requirements, https://www.registrarcorp.com/blog/cosmetics/mocra/cosmetics-serious-adverse-event-reporting/ — cross-check against FDA's own guidance page, https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-news-events/fda-issues-updated-instructions-serious-adverse-event-reporting-cosmetic-products.) **[UNCERTAIN]**: exact wording of what counts as a "serious adverse event" trigger should be double-checked against 21 CFR/FDA's MedWatch 3500A instructions if this ever comes up in practice.
- **Safety substantiation.** FDA's own fact sheet: "a manufacturer, packer or distributor whose name appears on the label... is required to ensure adequate substantiation of safety of their cosmetic product and to maintain records that support substantiation." This applies now, to everyone, small or not, and there is no registration exemption for it. Acceptable substantiation includes supplier safety data sheets/COAs, published toxicology literature, and Cosmetic Ingredient Review (CIR) panel findings — animal testing is not required.
- **Responsible-person label contact info** — in force since **December 29, 2024**: cosmetic labels must show the responsible person's domestic address, phone number, or electronic contact (e.g., a website contact form), specifically so consumers/FDA can report adverse events. **Action item:** confirm her tin/bottle/bag labels (not just the website) actually carry this — a PO box alone does not satisfy the separate, older FPLA name-and-place-of-business requirement, though it can work for the *MoCRA* responsible-person contact channel if paired with an electronic contact option. **[UNCERTAIN — recommend she personally verify her physical labels, since this task only reviewed the website copy, not label photos.]**

**Fragrance allergen labeling** — **not yet in effect.** FDA missed its statutory Dec 29, 2025 deadline; a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking is now expected around **May 2026**, with any final requirement phased in later (2027+ per current industry tracking of the Unified Agenda). No action required in 2026 beyond staying aware, since her ingredient lists already disclose "Essential Oils (Lavender, Cedarwood, etc.)" collectively, which is still allowed. (Multiple 2026 legal-industry summaries, e.g., Registrar Corp "MoCRA Unified Agenda" analysis; primary confirmation would come from a Federal Register NPRM once published — **[UNCERTAIN]** exact date, treat May 2026 as an estimate from secondary sources, not an FDA-published date I could independently verify.)

**Cosmetic GMP rule** — **not yet final.** MoCRA required FDA to issue GMP regulations by Dec 29, 2025; as of 2026 no final rule has issued, and industry trackers report the proposed rule has been pushed to FDA's "long-term actions" list (Foley & Lardner, "How MoCRA Is Reshaping FDA Oversight of Cosmetics in 2026," https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/03/how-mocra-is-reshaping-fda-oversight-of-cosmetics-in-2026/). Small businesses are also expected to have a GMP exemption once the rule issues (subject to the same exception categories above). **No 2026 action required**, though **[OPINION]** informally keeping basic batch records (date made, lot/batch number, ingredients used, quantities produced) costs nothing now and pays off if GMP or an adverse-event investigation ever asks for it.

## 3. Labelling: FPLA / 21 CFR 701 + web-copy checklist

**[LAW]** Per FDA's cosmetics labeling summary and its Small Business fact sheet (https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-labeling-regulations/summary-cosmetics-labeling-requirements and the fact sheet cited above):
- **Principal Display Panel (front of package):** statement of identity (what the product *is* — "body salve," "bath soak," not just the fanciful brand name), and net quantity of contents (weight/volume).
- **Information panel:** name and place of business of the manufacturer/packer/distributor — street address, city, state, ZIP (street address may be omitted only if the firm is listed in a current phone/city directory). **A P.O. box or website alone does not satisfy this** (explicit FDA answer in the fact sheet, Q11).
- **Ingredient declaration**, 21 CFR 701.3: descending order of predominance, using INCI (International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients) names — not marketing names or "natural" descriptors mixed into the ingredient list itself. FDA specifically warns: *"Don't use terms such as 'natural' as part of an ingredient statement... ingredients must be listed by their common or usual names, without additional description."*
- **Warnings** where applicable under 21 CFR 740 (not confirmed as applicable to her specific product types beyond the general safety-substantiation warning — **[UNCERTAIN]**, worth a targeted check against 21 CFR 740.10–740.19 for any aerosol/spray-specific warning requirements given she sells sprays).

**Web copy vs. physical label — important nuance [LAW + OPINION]:** The FPLA/21 CFR labeling rules technically govern the *physical package label*. But FDA's own "intended use" doctrine (Sections 1 and 5 above) explicitly pulls in *website and advertising claims* when deciding whether a product is a drug — so tightening only the tin/jar label while leaving "eczema," "arthritis," or "insomnia" in the web copy or JSON keywords does **not** reduce her drug-claim exposure; if anything the website is the more visible, easier-to-screenshot evidence trail. Conversely, the *physical* ingredient list, net-quantity, and name/address requirements don't legally have to be duplicated verbatim on the product page — but doing so (as she already does for ingredients) is good transparency practice and is close to mandatory in practice on Etsy, which requires ingredient disclosure for handmade cosmetics/bath products.

## 4. Bug spray: EPA/FIFRA verdict

**[LAW]** A product that makes a pesticidal claim (repels/kills/controls insects) is regulated as a pesticide under FIFRA unless it qualifies for the **Section 25(b) "minimum risk" exemption** — which requires that **both** the active ingredients **and** the inert/carrier ingredients appear on EPA's approved lists (40 CFR 152.25(f), Tables 1 and 2), and that the label make no false/misleading claims (40 CFR 156.10(a)(5)). Products meeting 25(b) don't need EPA registration, but a state may still require its own registration (EPA, "Minimum Risk Pesticides," https://www.epa.gov/minimum-risk-pesticides/minimum-risk-pesticides-inert-ingredient-and-active-ingredient-eligibility; AAPCO 25(b) label guidance PDF, https://aapco.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/25b_label.guidance_12.16.21.pdf).

**EPA's 25(b) approved active ingredient list** includes: citronella/citronella oil, cedar oil, cinnamon oil, clove oil, eugenol, garlic oil, geraniol, geranium oil, lemongrass oil, mint/peppermint oil, rosemary oil, thyme oil, soybean oil, and a short list of others (Cornell/AAPCO compiled list, https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/3b1a195d-b17b-43ad-aad0-5d1a04d82cff/content).

**Her bug spray's ingredient list (`bug-spray` in products.json):** *Alcohol, Glycerin, Germall Plus (Preservative), Essential Oils (Citronella, Lemongrass, Peppermint, Tea Tree, Lavender, Rosemary).*

**Verdict — [OPINION/analysis built on the LAW cited above], flagged uncertain where noted:**
- ✅ Citronella, Lemongrass, Peppermint, Rosemary are on EPA's 25(b) active ingredient list.
- ❌ **Tea Tree oil and Lavender oil do not appear on EPA's 25(b) approved active ingredient list** in any source I found. Including them as functional essential oils in a product marketed for insect defense is a real compliance gap, not a technicality — if they're part of what's being sold as "the blend that repels bugs," the whole formula likely falls outside the exemption. **[UNCERTAIN: confirm against the live, current eCFR Table 1 text (40 CFR 152.25(f)) or EPA's InertFinder tool, since lists are occasionally amended — I worked from several 2020s secondary compilations plus EPA's own overview page, not a fresh pull of the current CFR table text itself.]**
- ❌ **Germall Plus (diazolidinyl urea + iodopropynyl butylcarbamate) does not appear on EPA's approved inert-ingredient list (40 CFR 152.25(f), Table 2)** — Table 2 is a closed list, and an inert not on it disqualifies the product from the 25(b) exemption regardless of the actives used. **[Same uncertainty caveat as above — verify against the live CFR table/InertFinder before treating this as final.]**
- "Alcohol" and "Glycerin" as generic ingredient names are ambiguous (ethanol vs. isopropyl vs. denatured); isopropyl alcohol is confirmed on the approved inert list, and glycerin is commonly used in 25(b) formulations, but the vague labeling ("Alcohol") makes this unverifiable from the data available. **[UNCERTAIN]**

**Bottom line:** as currently formulated and as currently marketed (*"Tell the mosquitoes to buzz off, naturally,"* keywords "mosquito repellent," "ticks," "insect defense"), this product very likely does **not** qualify for the 25(b) minimum-risk exemption because of the preservative and (probably) two of the six essential oils — meaning it's making pesticidal claims without either EPA registration or a valid exemption. **[OPINION]** Two realistic paths: (1) reformulate to an all-25(b)-eligible ingredient set (drop or replace tea tree/lavender, swap Germall Plus for an EPA-listed inert or restructure the claim so the preservative isn't functionally "in" the pesticide product), or (2) stop making insect-repellency claims altogether and reposition it as a scented body/outdoor mist without an efficacy promise — which sidesteps FIFRA entirely since a product is only regulated as a pesticide if it makes a pesticidal claim.

**Separately, an FTC substantiation problem exists regardless of FIFRA status:** the CDC/EPA position is that "products containing natural oils haven't been shown to be effective at repelling insects," and the CDC does not recommend pure/unformulated essential oils (including tea tree) as repellents, in contrast to registered actives (DEET, picaridin, oil of lemon eucalyptus/PMD, IR3535) which have validated efficacy data (CDC Yellow Book / NPIC summary via search). Any "repels mosquitoes" style claim needs its own competent-and-reliable-evidence backing under FTC rules independent of the EPA registration question — currently unsubstantiated. **[Recommend: soften to non-efficacy language like "our go-to for bug season" rather than "tell the mosquitoes to buzz off," unless/until she has real repellency data.]**

DEET/picaridin comparison for her own reference: DEET is the most-studied and most broadly effective; picaridin (20%) rivals it for 8–14 hrs; oil of lemon eucalyptus (PMD 20–30%) is comparable at shorter duration; none of these are what's in her formula (search results, REI/NPIC/CDC Yellow Book summaries).

## 5. Aromatherapy, room sprays, and the "protection keychain"

**[LAW]** Restating FDA's Aromatherapy guidance directly (https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/aromatherapy): intended use is what controls. A fragrance "intended only to make a person smell good" is a cosmetic. The same scent marketed with a claim it will "help the consumer sleep or quit smoking... meets the definition of a drug." Room/ambient fragrance products with **no bodily-application intent and no health claim** (air fresheners, scented candles) fall under the **Consumer Product Safety Commission**, not FDA, per the same page.

Applying that to her ritual line:
- **Cleansing Spray / Porch Sweep Spray** — currently framed as folk/spiritual "clear the energy," "clear stuck energy," explicitly *not* medical ("no more landlord complaints," "safe around pets when dry"). This framing is meaningfully different from a health claim and, per FDA's own logic, likely keeps these as cosmetics/room fragrances (or CPSC-territory ambient fragrance products) rather than drugs — **[OPINION]**, since FDA hasn't published anything specifically addressing "energy clearing" language, this is an inference from the cosmetic-vs-drug framework, not a direct FDA statement. The risk would rise sharply if the copy ever shifted to "reduces stress," "eases anxiety after a hard day," or similar — that's the exact line FDA draws.
- **Protection Potion Keychain** — already labeled *"non-topical... not for consumption or topical skin application"* in its own `usageGuide.patchTest` field, which is good practice; it's a sealed, non-topical curio, not a cosmetic under FDA's "applied to the human body" definition, and its "protection" framing is spiritual/talismanic rather than a claim to prevent illness or injury, so it sits outside FDA's cosmetic/drug jurisdiction as currently worded. **[OPINION]** Keep it explicitly non-topical and don't let "protection" drift toward implying physical safety (e.g., injury/illness prevention) — that would raise a different (FTC deceptive-claim) issue, not an FDA cosmetic one.

## 6. Shipping and selling

**USPS / carrier hazmat [LAW, with product-specific application flagged UNCERTAIN]:** Alcohol-based fragrance/spray products are DOT Class 3 flammable liquids. Her **Bug Spray** is the clearest candidate for this (listed ingredient "Alcohol"); Cleansing Spray and Porch Sweep Spray use "Witch Hazel," which is typically a low-alcohol (~14%) product — likely below flammable-liquid thresholds but **[UNCERTAIN — depends on her supplier's specific witch hazel formulation/flash point, which isn't in the product data]**. **Action: pull the SDS/flash point for each spray's actual alcohol content from her suppliers and confirm with USPS/UPS whether Limited Quantity marking and ground-only shipping apply**, since this determination can't be made from ingredient names alone.

**SC sales tax on shipping [LAW]:** South Carolina taxes shipping/handling charges when they're for taxable goods and the charge is mandatory or bundled into the price; a separately-stated, customer-optional shipping charge can be exempt (TaxJar/Avalara SC guides). All her products are taxable tangible personal property, so if shipping is a flat included charge (as her free-shipping-over-$40 structure suggests it functions), it's very likely taxable when charged. **[Recommend confirming current treatment directly with SCDOR (dor.sc.gov) or her sales-tax platform, since South Carolina's exact "separately stated + optional" test has nuances this task didn't fully verify against SCDOR's own regulations.]**

**SC cottage/home-based business requirements for cosmetics [LAW + UNCERTAIN]:** Unlike food (which has cottage-food carve-outs), I found **no South Carolina-specific cosmetics manufacturing license or DHEC cosmetics permit** — cosmetics manufacturing in SC appears to fall under general state/local business licensing rather than a dedicated cosmetics regime, consistent with FDA's own fact sheet answer that "FDA does not license cosmetics firms... state or local authorities may require licensing." What does apply generically: a South Carolina retail license from SCDOR for taxable sales, and a local (Landrum/Spartanburg County) business license plus zoning sign-off for operating a home-based manufacturing business. **[UNCERTAIN — I could not find a definitive SC DHEC cosmetics-specific rule either way; recommend a direct call to SCDOR and Spartanburg County/Landrum zoning/business-license office to confirm nothing cosmetics-specific applies to her home kitchen/workshop setup.]**

## 7. Other claims: FTC, Prop 65, allergens

**FTC substantiation standards [LAW]:** Every claim must be truthful, not misleading, and backed by evidence appropriate to the claim (FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance, https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Health-Products-Compliance-Guidance.pdf).
- **"Handmade"/"small batch"** — factual, objective claims; fine as long as they're literally true (they appear to be, given her described one-person Landrum operation).
- **"Dermatologist tested"** — not currently used anywhere in her product data reviewed; flagging per the task's request: if ever added, this phrase legally requires *some* real substantiation behind it (even a single dermatologist's formula review is sometimes treated as minimally sufficient, but a specific claim like "clinically proven" needs an actual study) — don't add it without something to point to.
- **"Hypoallergenic"** — also not currently used; same caution: FDA doesn't define or verify the term, but FTC can still challenge it as misleading if unsubstantiated, especially when paired with specific statistics.
- **"Cruelty-free"** — not currently used as a claim (though several SKUs are tagged "vegan," which is a distinct, ingredient-based claim, not an animal-testing claim); if ever added, needs some basis (supplier attestations, no in-house animal testing, etc.).
- **"All natural"/"chemical-free"/"preservative-free"** — see Section 3 checklist above; her FAQ's blanket "all-natural and preservative-free" line appears to conflict with several actual formulas (Optiphen, Germall Plus, synthetic fragrance) and is the single most concrete, fixable FTC-exposure item found in this review.

**Prop 65 (California) [LAW]:** Businesses with **fewer than 10 employees** are exempt from Prop 65's warning-label requirement (p65warnings.ca.gov FAQ). As a one-person operation, she's almost certainly under that threshold and likely **exempt** from needing Prop 65 warnings on her own site. **[UNCERTAIN caveat]**: if she sells through Amazon or a marketplace that imposes its own Prop 65 compliance policy independent of the legal employee-count exemption, that platform's rules could still require action regardless of her actual legal exposure — worth checking Etsy's seller policies specifically, since that's her stated second channel.

**Allergen/pregnancy warnings [OPINION — best practice, not a specific cited legal mandate beyond the general safety-substantiation duty in Section 2]:** She already discloses the almond-oil/tree-nut risk in Frankincense Salve's `ingredientsNote` and `usageGuide.patchTest` — good practice, and this satisfies part of her general MoCRA safety-substantiation duty by showing she's identified and warned about a known allergen. Recommend extending similar explicit warnings to: essential-oil-heavy products (Sleep Salve, Bug Spray, Backroad Soak with peppermint/eucalyptus, Cleansing/Porch Sweep sprays with sage/palo santo) regarding use on children and during pregnancy, since several common essential oils (rosemary, sage, peppermint in concentrated form) carry conventional caution advice for those groups. This isn't a specific FDA-mandated label element for cosmetics, but it directly supports the "adequate substantiation of safety" duty FDA already imposes, and reduces real-world adverse-event/liability exposure.
